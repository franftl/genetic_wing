# UAV FP — Research Note 2: One-Year Feasibility & AI Leak-Detection Difficulty

*Compiled 12 August 2026. Follow-up to Research Note 1.*

## Bottom line

Building a working, demonstrable proof-of-concept in a year is realistic **if you scope it correctly** — waypoint-autonomous flight over a wellsite with a thermal/OGI camera feeding an AI detector is well within reach for a 3-person mechanical engineering team using commodity, off-the-shelf building blocks. Full "drone-in-a-box," 24/7, BVLOS, fully unattended operation — the version the commercial players (Percepto, Skydio) sell — is not realistic to build from scratch in a year, and shouldn't be the year-1 target. The AI leak-detection part specifically is *less* of a bottleneck than people expect, **provided you get your hands on a real OGI camera** — that access, not the machine learning, is the actual constraint.

## 1. How hard is the AI leak-detection part, really?

Two things make this easier than it sounds:

**The camera does most of the work before the AI ever sees the video.** Optical Gas Imaging (OGI) cameras — like the Sierra Olympia Ventus OGI or FLIR's G-series — are tuned to the mid-wave infrared absorption band of hydrocarbon gases (methane, propane, etc.). A leak is already visible to the human eye in the video feed as a smoke-like plume ("Gas Enhancement Mode," as Sierra Olympia calls it). That means the AI task isn't "infer an invisible physical phenomenon from raw data" — it's closer to a solved computer-vision problem: video-based smoke/plume/motion detection, a domain with decades of prior work to borrow from.

**Published results back this up.** A Stanford group built VideoGasNet, training 2D-CNN, 3D-CNN, and ConvLSTM architectures on their own "GasVid" dataset — about 0.7 million labeled video frames of real methane leaks across a wide range of leak sizes and camera distances. Their simple binary task — *is there a leak in this clip, yes or no* — hit **near-100% accuracy**. That's the task that matters for your thesis. Things get much harder only when you push further: classifying leak size into 3 categories dropped accuracy to 78%, and 8-category classification (fine-grained leak-rate estimation) fell to 39%. A separate 2025 paper (infrared gas-leak detection with an improved YOLO network) reports similar patterns — strong detection performance (AP50 up to 96%) once background interference and low signal-to-noise plumes are handled, but flags wind, dust, complex backgrounds, and uncooled-sensor noise as the recurring practical headaches, not the modeling itself.

**Translating that to your thesis scope:** aim for binary/localized detection ("there is a leak, and it's roughly here in the frame") rather than leak-rate quantification or gas-type classification. That's the achievable, still-genuinely-useful target, and it's the one the literature says is close to solved with a decent camera and a reasonably-sized dataset. You don't need to train a network from scratch either — transfer learning from a pretrained video/action-recognition backbone (standard practice, a few days to a couple weeks of work for someone learning it fresh) plus fine-tuning on whatever real or public gas-leak footage you can get will very likely outperform anything built from zero.

**Where the real difficulty hides: data, not modeling.** You won't have Stanford's 0.7-million-frame proprietary dataset. Realistic paths to training data, roughly in order of practicality: (1) get real leak footage from a sponsor — even a modest amount of supervised/controlled-release footage from an actual site visit would be extremely valuable and is exactly the kind of thing a sponsor conversation should ask for; (2) use published datasets and pretrained models as a base and fine-tune; (3) generate synthetic/simulated gas-plume overlays on real thermal background footage — a legitimate, commonly-used technique when real leak events are rare or unsafe to stage; (4) partner-of-convenience with a controlled release test (some regulators/operators run scheduled controlled releases specifically to validate detection equipment — worth asking about). Any of these is far more of a bottleneck than the neural network architecture itself.

## 2. The camera is the actual gatekeeper, and it's expensive and hard to source

This is the single biggest risk to the whole project, more than the software. OGI cameras capable of visualizing hydrocarbon gas plumes (Sierra Olympia Ventus OGI, FLIR's G-series) are specialized, quote-only industrial instruments — none of the vendors publish list prices, which itself is a signal (this class of hardware has historically run in the tens of thousands of USD, consistent with FLIR's G-series being priced and marketed as professional LDAR/compliance equipment, not consumer gear). The Ventus OGI is drone-payload-sized and light enough to matter for you (541–570 g, 12 W typical draw, standard tripod/M3 mounting — genuinely designed to be flown), but getting one into your hands as a student team almost certainly means borrowing, renting, or having a sponsor provide it rather than purchasing outright. This should be one of the very first things you ask a prospective sponsor for — access to a camera (even short-term/loaner) is probably worth more to your project timeline than cash.

Cheaper uncooled thermal cameras (the kind used for general heat-loss/insulation inspection, security, or fire detection — not certified gas-imaging) are much more affordable and widely available, but they are a fundamentally different sensor: they show *temperature*, not gas concentration, and won't reliably visualize a methane plume the way an OGI camera does. If camera access turns out to be the blocker, pivoting the thermal angle toward equipment overheating/fire-risk detection (a real need per Research Note 1) rather than gas-plume detection is a legitimate fallback that uses much cheaper, easier-to-source hardware.

## 3. The flight/autonomy stack is not the hard part

Waypoint-based autonomous flight — pre-programmed routes over a wellsite, automatic launch/land, geofencing — is a solved, commodity problem. Open-source autopilots (PX4, ArduPilot) plus a companion computer (Jetson, Raspberry Pi-class boards are commonly paired with them specifically for onboard vision tasks) are well documented, widely used in exactly this kind of student/research project, and don't require you to build anything novel. This is squarely within a mechanical/aerospace-adjacent engineering team's comfort zone and shouldn't eat much of your year.

What *is* genuinely hard, and is its own specialized hardware-engineering discipline: the **docking/charging station** that makes 24/7 "drone-in-a-box" autonomy possible (precision auto-landing, battery swap or induction charging, weatherproofing). Multiple companies sell this as a standalone product because it's non-trivial mechatronics. Building one in year one, on top of everything else, is realistically out of scope — treat continuous unattended operation as future work/thesis "next steps," and demonstrate the core value (autonomous flight + leak detection) with a simpler manually-launched or scheduled-launch operating model instead.

## 4. Regulatory timeline runs in parallel, not in series

Per Research Note 1, BVLOS operation in Argentina requires ANAC's "Specific" category authorization — a documented risk-mitigation case and a Work Aerial Operator Certificate for commercial-type operation. This is paperwork and process time, not R&D time, so it can and should run in parallel with hardware/software development rather than gating it. For thesis purposes, note that a fully rigorous BVLOS-authorized commercial-grade operation may not even be necessary to demonstrate the concept — flight tests within visual line of sight, in a rural area (which Vaca Muerta onshore sites generally are), fall under the much lighter Open category and would let you validate the actual technical questions this year while treating full BVLOS authorization as part of the path to a real deployed product rather than a year-1 requirement.

## 5. A realistic one-year shape

Roughly, and assuming things can overlap: the first couple of months go to locking down scope, securing a sponsor relationship (critically, for camera access and possibly site access), and picking a COTS drone platform rather than building an airframe from scratch. The middle stretch — call it months 3 through 7 or 8 — is where the real engineering happens in parallel tracks: integrating the camera and companion computer onto the airframe and getting reliable waypoint autonomy working on one track, while the AI/data track works through sourcing or generating training footage and getting a fine-tuned detection model running (first on recorded video, then closer to real-time). BVLOS/regulatory paperwork can be moving in the background this whole time. The last few months are for field testing at a real or realistic site, iterating on false-positive rates, and pulling it together for the thesis writeup and defense. The riskiest single dependency in that whole shape is camera access — it's worth trying to lock that down in month one, since everything on the AI side is downstream of having real gas-imaging footage to work with.
