# UAV FP — Research Note 3: How to Actually Train the AI, and Can a Cheaper Camera Do the Job?

*Compiled 12 August 2026. Follow-up to Research Notes 1–2.*

## Short answer to both questions

Training the model is the approachable part — it follows a well-documented, tutorial-level workflow (transfer learning on a modern object detector, using free/standard tools) that a team without a deep ML background can realistically pick up in weeks, not months. The camera question has a more interesting answer than "yes or no": a **generic** affordable thermal camera physically cannot see gas — that's not a budget/AI problem, it's optics. But there's a real middle category, **purpose-built uncooled OGI cameras**, that are meaningfully cheaper than the premium cooled cameras from Research Note 2 and are genuinely capable of the detection task, just at somewhat reduced sensitivity. That's the camera you should be asking a sponsor for if a top-tier cooled unit isn't available.

## 1. Could a cheaper thermal camera do the detection work?

**A plain/generic thermal camera — no.** This isn't a close call. Standard thermal cameras (the kind used for building inspections, security, or consumer drones) operate across a broad long-wave infrared band and are built to image surface temperature, not gas concentration. Detecting a gas optically requires narrow spectral filtering — on the order of 0.1–0.3 μm bandwidth — tuned to a specific absorption feature of the target gas (methane's is around 3.2–3.55 μm in the mid-wave band, or a different fingerprint region around 7.2–8.3 μm depending on the technology). A generic thermal sensor has no such filter, so a gas cloud simply doesn't register — it's invisible to the sensor by design, not by insufficient training data. The one caveat is *indirect* detection: a high-pressure leak escaping through a small orifice can cause visible cooling on nearby surfaces via the Joule-Thomson effect (or frost, for cryogenic releases), and a generic thermal camera can see that temperature change. But that's detecting a side effect under fairly specific conditions (high pressure differential, visible surface nearby), not the general-purpose leak detection you'd want for routine well monitoring — it won't reliably catch a slow seep at ambient conditions, which is the more common real-world case.

**A purpose-built uncooled OGI camera — yes, with a real tradeoff.** This is the useful middle ground, and it exists today as an actual product category, not a hypothetical. FLIR's GF77a, for instance, is explicitly marketed as "the first methane gas detection camera that utilizes a much more affordable, uncooled detector technology," at roughly half the cost of FLIR's cooled G-series. Academic work backs this up with numbers: a 2026 comparison study found an uncooled microbolometer-based OGI setup (narrowband-filtered to the 7.2–8.3 μm methane band) reaches about 85 mK thermal noise versus 22 mK for a cooled mid-wave camera — meaningfully noisier, and needing a faster (more light-gathering) lens to compensate — but the authors' conclusion was that modern uncooled cameras have gotten good enough to meet the EU's "Type 1" LDAR compliance standard (detecting leaks down to about 17 g/h), even though they fall short of the stricter "Type 2" threshold (1 g/h) that only cooled cameras reliably hit. Put concretely: an uncooled, purpose-built OGI camera will reliably catch a real, meaningful leak; it just won't catch the smallest, earliest-stage ones the way a $30–80k cooled instrument would, and it generally can't do precise leak-rate quantification the way cooled MWIR cameras can.

**What this means for your thesis:** the camera you want to ask a sponsor for isn't necessarily the top-of-the-line cooled Sierra Olympia Ventus or FLIR GF320 — a purpose-built uncooled OGI unit (FLIR's GF77a-class, or equivalent) gets you a working, credible detection capability at meaningfully lower cost, and is a more realistic ask for a student project. What you should avoid is substituting in a generic (non-gas) thermal camera and hoping the AI compensates — no amount of training fixes a sensor that isn't physically registering the thing you're trying to detect. If cost still doesn't work out even for the uncooled OGI category, that's the point where pivoting the thermal angle toward equipment overheating/fire-risk detection (which any decent thermal camera *can* do) becomes the more honest fallback, as flagged in Research Note 2.

## 2. How would you actually go about training the model?

This breaks into a fairly standard applied-ML workflow. None of the individual steps are research-frontier — they're well-trodden, documented, and supported by mature open-source tooling. Here's the practical path:

**Scope the task narrowly first.** Per Research Note 2, aim for binary/localized detection — "is there a plume visible in this frame, and roughly where" — rather than leak-rate quantification or gas-type classification. This is both the achievable target and the one that maps directly onto what an OGI camera's "gas enhancement mode" already gives you visually.

**Get or generate labeled data.** In rough order of practicality: real footage from a sponsor's controlled-release test or site visit is the gold standard and worth prioritizing in early sponsor conversations; failing that, generating synthetic training examples by compositing simulated gas-plume renders onto real thermal background footage is a legitimate and commonly used technique in this field when real leak events are scarce or unsafe to stage on demand; some published datasets or pretrained models may also be available on request from the authors of papers like the Stanford VideoGasNet work, even if not fully public.

**Label it with standard tools, not custom software.** CVAT (Computer Vision Annotation Tool) and Roboflow are the two most common free/open tools for exactly this kind of video/image annotation — drawing bounding boxes or masks around the visible plume frame by frame — and both integrate directly with common training pipelines, so you're not building labeling infrastructure from scratch.

**Fine-tune an existing detector rather than designing one from scratch.** This is the key point that makes the "AI part" much less daunting than it sounds: modern object detectors like the YOLO family (widely used via the Ultralytics framework) come pretrained on large general datasets, and fine-tuning them on a small custom dataset via transfer learning is the standard approach — well documented, with step-by-step tutorials specifically for this workflow. Because you're fine-tuning rather than training from zero, you don't need Stanford's 700,000-frame dataset; a few hundred to low thousands of well-labeled frames, combined with data augmentation, is a realistic starting point for a working proof-of-concept, even if it won't match published multi-class accuracy numbers.

**Training compute is modest.** Fine-tuning a YOLO-class model on a dataset this size doesn't require a supercomputer — a single prosumer GPU, or even a free-tier cloud notebook (Google Colab-class), is generally enough, with training runs measured in hours, not days or weeks. This matters for a student budget and timeline.

**Validate deliberately for false positives.** The recurring challenge flagged across the literature reviewed in Research Note 2 isn't detecting an obvious plume — it's telling a real plume apart from wind-blown dust, heat shimmer, or background clutter. Build your test set specifically to include those confounders, not just clean positive/negative examples, and treat false-positive rate under realistic field conditions as a headline metric for your thesis evaluation, not just raw accuracy.

**Iterate with your own flight data.** Once you have an initial model, the highest-value activity is running it against footage from your own test flights and feeding back the cases it gets wrong — a lightweight, informal version of active learning that steadily improves robustness to your specific site conditions, camera, and typical false-positive sources, rather than trying to get everything right from a one-shot training pass.

**Decide on real-time vs. post-flight inference pragmatically.** Running inference onboard in real time (e.g., on a Jetson-class companion computer) is achievable but adds integration work; running the trained model on downloaded video after each flight is functionally simpler and, for a first working demonstration, perfectly sufficient to prove the concept — real-time onboard inference can be framed as a stretch goal rather than a baseline requirement.

## Net assessment

Nothing in this pipeline is "hard" in the sense of requiring novel research — it's closer to "unfamiliar but well-documented," which for a mechanical engineering team means a real but manageable learning curve, best tackled early (start the ML track in parallel with hardware integration, not after it). The genuine risk items remain what they were in Research Note 2: getting your hands on the right camera (now clarified: a purpose-built uncooled OGI unit is a realistic, meaningfully cheaper ask than a cooled one) and getting enough real or realistic training footage to validate against — both of which point back to the same conclusion, that an early, concrete ask to a prospective sponsor for camera access and/or a controlled test event is worth more to this project than almost anything else you could spend time on right now.
