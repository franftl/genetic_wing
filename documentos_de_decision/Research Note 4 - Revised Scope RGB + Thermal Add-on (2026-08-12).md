# UAV FP — Research Note 4: Revised Scope (RGB Personnel/Vehicle Monitoring + Documentation, Optional Thermal)

*Compiled 12 August 2026. Follow-up to Research Notes 1–3, reflecting the decision to drop gas-specific OGI imaging as cost-prohibitive.*

## Short answer

This is a sound pivot, and in some ways an easier sell than the methane angle. There's an established commercial market already selling exactly this — autonomous drones for perimeter security, unauthorized-access detection, and vehicle/personnel tracking at oil & gas sites — and the underlying AI task (detecting people and vehicles from the air) is a mature, well-resourced computer vision problem with purpose-built public datasets, unlike gas-plume detection where the hard part was data scarcity. Photographing/documenting wellheads is also already a mainstream, well-precedented drone use case in the industry. Adding a general (non-gas) thermal camera as a second sensor is now genuinely affordable — a completely different price tier than the OGI cameras discussed in Notes 2–3, because you no longer need the specialized narrowband gas-absorption filtering, just a standard cooled-or-uncooled LWIR sensor.

## 1. The security/monitoring angle is real and already commercialized

Asylon Robotics runs an autonomous drone security product specifically for oil & gas critical infrastructure — wellheads, pump stations, tank farms, pipeline corridors — detecting unauthorized entry, equipment theft, restricted-area breaches, and activity near hazardous zones. It's not fully autonomous end-to-end: their "Guardian" drone flies automated patrols but routes final incident verification and response decisions through a 24/7 human-staffed operations center, which is a sensible and achievable autonomy target for you too (autonomous detection and flagging, human makes the call) rather than a fully closed-loop autonomous response system. They also frame their offering around compliance documentation (NERC CIP-006, TSA pipeline standards, API 780 risk assessments) — a useful reminder that "security footage with timestamps" is itself a sellable deliverable, not just raw detection.

This also lines up with what YPF itself said about its own drone program in Research Note 1 — the RTIC's drones are explicitly described as detecting "situaciones de seguridad o de producción" (safety or production issues), which is closer to this revised scope than to the gas-detection angle. That's good news (it confirms real demand) and a useful reality check (YPF is already doing some version of this in-house, so your differentiation angle should probably lean toward operators who don't have YPF's budget for a purpose-built RTIC — smaller independent Vaca Muerta producers are still a reasonable first-sponsor target per Note 1).

## 2. The AI task got dramatically easier — this is the good news of the pivot

Person and vehicle detection from aerial imagery is one of the best-resourced problems in computer vision today, in sharp contrast to gas-plume detection where the real bottleneck was data scarcity (Notes 2–3). There's a purpose-built public dataset for exactly your use case: **VisDrone**, built by Tianjin University's AISKYEYE team specifically for drone-based detection — 8,629+ images (6,471 train / 548 validation / 1,610 test) shot from actual drones across 14 cities, labeled across 10 classes that map almost exactly onto what you need (pedestrian, people, car, van, truck, bus, motor, bicycle, tricycle). It's freely available, integrates directly with the standard Ultralytics YOLO training pipeline (a few lines of Python download and train it automatically), and is explicitly built to handle the hard part of aerial imagery — small, dense objects seen from altitude — which is the one genuine technical challenge here rather than data availability.

Practically, this means you can have a working baseline detector running almost immediately using a general pretrained model (COCO-pretrained detectors already recognize "person" and "car" reasonably well out of the box) and then spend your engineering time fine-tuning on VisDrone plus your own flight footage for the aerial-specific quirks — altitude, angle, small object size, occlusion — rather than starting from nothing or hunting for scarce proprietary data the way the gas-detection track would have required. This is a meaningfully lower-risk AI track than the one in Notes 2–3.

The wellhead-photography/documentation piece is even less of a stretch technically — it's closer to "fly a route, take pictures, flag visible changes between visits" than to real-time detection, and is already a mainstream offering across the industry (per Research Note 1: Percepto, Skydio, Cyberhawk, and others all sell general visual inspection as a core product, well before you get to gas-specific sensing).

## 3. A general thermal camera is now a genuinely affordable add-on

This is the key difference from Notes 2–3: once you drop the requirement for gas-specific spectral filtering, thermal imaging stops being a $15,000–$80,000+ proposition and becomes a normal commercial-drone accessory. A standard uncooled LWIR thermal sensor is useful here for two things — nighttime/low-visibility personnel and vehicle detection (thermal typically outperforms RGB after dark, which matters for a security-monitoring use case) and basic equipment overheating/fire-risk detection (a legitimate secondary use flagged back in Note 1). Roughly, in order of integration effort versus cost: a raw thermal camera core like FLIR's Boson 640 (640×512, available down to a few grams for the core itself) runs around $3,500 and is meant for exactly this kind of custom integration onto a small team's own airframe, though it requires you to do the mounting/integration work yourselves and comes with export-control paperwork (an End Use Statement) to account for in your timeline. If you'd rather buy an integrated, ready-to-fly solution instead of engineering your own mount, DJI's Mavic 3T is a consumer/prosumer drone with a built-in RGB+thermal camera around $6,800, and DJI's professional Zenmuse-series gimbal payloads (RGB + thermal + zoom + laser rangefinder, the kind of payload used on larger enterprise platforms) run in the $11,000–$12,000 range. All three of those are a different order of magnitude from the OGI cameras in Notes 2–3, and the cheapest of them (the Boson core) is plausibly within reach of a sponsor's in-kind or cash contribution rather than requiring a major corporate loan of specialized equipment.

## 4. What this trades off against the earlier gas-detection framing

Worth being explicit about one thing: this pivot moves you away from the single sharpest regulatory hook identified in Research Note 1 — Neuquén's methane-reporting deadline hitting next month. Security and documentation don't have that same forced-compliance urgency behind them. What they do have is a more evergreen, always-relevant pain point for remote onshore operators: theft and vandalism at unmanned or lightly-staffed wellsites, lone-worker safety, and the sheer logistical cost of physically visiting thousands of dispersed wells just to confirm nothing's wrong — all of which apply regardless of any specific emissions regulation, and all of which you already have real evidence (Asylon's product line, YPF's own stated use of drones for safety/production issue detection) that operators will actually pay for.

## Net effect on the year-1 plan from Research Note 2

This pivot makes the AI track meaningfully lower-risk than it was under the gas-detection scope — you're no longer gated on scarce, hard-to-obtain real leak footage, since VisDrone plus your own flight data gets you a working detector much faster. The camera-sourcing risk drops sharply too, since you're now shopping in the $3,500–$12,000 range instead of tens of thousands. If anything, this suggests the smarter year-1 sequencing is: get RGB detection (people/vehicles) and route-based photo documentation working first, since that's the most de-risked path end-to-end, then treat the thermal camera as a stretch addition once budget/sponsor support is confirmed rather than a day-one requirement.
