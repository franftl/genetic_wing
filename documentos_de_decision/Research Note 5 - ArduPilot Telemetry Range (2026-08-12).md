# UAV FP — Research Note 5: Long-Range Telemetry with ArduPilot, and Mission Behavior on Telemetry Loss

*Compiled 12 August 2026. Follow-up to Research Notes 1–4.*

## Short answer

"Ultra-high range" telemetry isn't really one solution — it's radio telemetry for the tens-of-kilometers tier, cellular for effectively unlimited range wherever there's coverage, and satellite as the true no-gaps fallback, usually combined rather than chosen exclusively. And yes, a mission can complete after telemetry is lost — but this is not ArduPilot's out-of-the-box default. Out of the box, losing the ground link is treated as a fault and the aircraft aborts its mission to fly home; continuing the mission through a telemetry dropout is a deliberate configuration choice you have to make (one parameter, well documented, commonly used for exactly this kind of long-range autonomous survey work).

## 1. Getting long range: three tiers, and they're usually stacked, not chosen one-or-the-other

**Radio telemetry (tens of km, line-of-sight-limited).** This is the standard ArduPilot telemetry link — a 900 MHz radio pair like the RFD900/RFD900x, which ArduPilot's own documentation describes as reaching "long range >40km depending on antennas and GCS setup," with 15 km+ commonly reported in typical use. The catch is that this is fundamentally a line-of-sight radio link — range depends heavily on terrain, antenna height/gain, and obstructions, not just transmit power. One practical wrinkle worth flagging early: many flight controllers' telemetry ports can't supply enough current to run these radios at full transmit power, so achieving the upper end of the range figures typically means powering the radio externally rather than straight off the autopilot. The Patagonian steppe terrain around most onshore Vaca Muerta sites is relatively flat and open, which is favorable for this kind of line-of-sight link compared to, say, forested or mountainous terrain — worth factoring into your range expectations positively.

**Cellular telemetry (effectively unlimited range, coverage-dependent).** ArduPilot officially documents this as a supported approach (UAVCast, running MAVLink over a 3G/4G modem), explicitly framed as covering "nearly unlimited distances" versus a radio's line-of-sight ceiling. The real-world catch, and it's a significant one for a rural oil field: cell tower antennas are physically tilted to cover ground-level users, not aircraft altitude, so coverage can actually get *worse* as the drone climbs, even well within a tower's normal ground-level range — ArduPilot's own docs flag this specifically. Cellular also depends entirely on infrastructure being present at all, which brings us to the most telling data point from your earlier research: YPF's own Vaca Muerta monitoring center (Research Note 1) uses **Starlink**, not cellular, for its connectivity. That's a strong signal that cell coverage across the actual field is inconsistent enough that a major operator with resources chose satellite internet instead — worth treating as a realistic expectation for your own project rather than assuming cellular will just work everywhere.

**Satellite telemetry (truly global, low-bandwidth).** Iridium-based links (the class of hardware used for this is typically marketed for exactly this "lost-link safety net" role) offer genuinely global coverage independent of ground infrastructure, but at low bandwidth — they're generally used for status/position reporting and emergency command (e.g., "return home now") rather than a full live MAVLink telemetry stream with video. This is the right tool for "I need to know the aircraft is safe and be able to recall it from literally anywhere," not for continuous high-rate monitoring.

**In practice, real long-range operations combine these** rather than betting everything on one link: radio for primary telemetry near the operator/dock, with cellular or satellite as a backup or extended-range channel, sometimes with a relay approach (a second aircraft, an elevated ground repeater, or a vehicle-mounted relay station) to extend line-of-sight radio range incrementally when infrastructure-based options aren't reliable. Given Vaca Muerta's scale and YPF's own choice of Starlink, a two-tier approach — radio telemetry for normal site-local operation, satellite or Starlink-based backup for connectivity assurance over the wider field — is probably the more realistic target for your project than trying to engineer one link to do everything.

## 2. Can the mission complete if telemetry is lost? Yes — but you have to tell ArduPilot that's okay

This is the important nuance, and it's easy to get backwards. The autopilot itself doesn't need the ground link to keep flying — a mission uploaded to the flight controller runs onboard, independent of whether anyone's watching. But ArduPilot's **failsafe system** treats a lost ground-station heartbeat as a potential problem by default, and its out-of-the-box response is to abandon the mission and fly home, not to shrug and continue.

Specifically: ArduPilot Copter's GCS failsafe triggers once the telemetry heartbeat has been missing for a timeout period (5 seconds by default). The default failsafe action (`FS_GCS_ENABLE` = 1) is to switch to Return-to-Launch — or Land, if GPS isn't available. Critically, the official documentation is explicit that the vehicle does **not** automatically continue its pre-loaded mission when this triggers — that's a deliberate safety default, not an oversight, since in normal operation a lost ground link might mean something's actually wrong.

The fix is a single, well-documented parameter: `FS_OPTIONS`, bit 1, labeled "Continue if in AUTO mode on Ground Control Station Failsafe." With that bit set, a telemetry dropout while the aircraft is flying an autonomous mission no longer triggers RTL — it just keeps flying its programmed route, and you've lost your live view of it until the link comes back (or it finishes and lands per the mission's own final steps). This is standard, commonly-used practice for exactly the kind of long-range autonomous survey/mapping flights where a ground link drop mid-route is expected and shouldn't be treated as an emergency.

Two things worth keeping in mind when you configure this: first, there's an equivalent and independent setting for the RC control link specifically (`FS_OPTIONS` bit 0, "Continue if in auto mode on Radio Failsafe") — telemetry loss and RC-transmitter loss are two separate failsafes in ArduPilot, and for a genuinely autonomous, mission-driven operation you may not even need an RC transmitter link at all (ArduPilot has a documented "GCS-only operation" mode for exactly this). Second, and more important for a real deployment: you should generally leave the *other* failsafes — low battery, GPS/EKF failure, geofence breach — fully active even while disabling the GCS-specific one. Telling the aircraft "a lost ground link isn't itself an emergency" is very different from disabling its safety net entirely; the sensible configuration is narrowly permissive (continue through a comms dropout) while keeping every other independent safety check in force.

## Net takeaway for the thesis

Treat "range" and "mission resilience" as two separate design questions, because ArduPilot treats them that way. Range is a hardware/link-budget problem, solved with the radio/cellular/satellite stack above, sized to how far your docked drone actually needs to roam from its base. Mission resilience on a comms dropout is a one-parameter configuration decision (`FS_OPTIONS` bit 1) — cheap to implement, well precedented in real long-range ArduPilot deployments, and worth explicitly calling out in your thesis as a considered design choice (continue-on-GCS-loss, still abort-on-battery/GPS/geofence-failure) rather than something you stumbled into.
