# UAV FP — Research Note 6: Real-Time Visual Tracking of People/Vehicles and Feeding Positions to ArduPilot

*Compiled 12 August 2026. Follow-up to Research Notes 1–5.*

## Short answer

Yes to all three parts of the question. Real-time streaming of detections is standard practice, ArduPilot has documented, well-used mechanisms for a companion computer to redirect the aircraft in real time via MAVLink, and the "math" (it's closer to single-camera ray-ground geolocation refined over time than classic multi-observer triangulation) is a published, flight-tested technique, not experimental research territory. There's also a second, much simpler path worth knowing about: ArduPilot has a *built-in* follow capability for cooperative targets (something that broadcasts its own GPS position) that needs no computer vision or geolocation math at all — worth considering as a complementary or fallback approach alongside the harder vision-based one.

## 1. Two fundamentally different ways to "follow," worth separating clearly

**Cooperative tracking — the target broadcasts its own position; ArduPilot handles the rest natively.** ArduPilot Copter ships with a "Follow Mode" designed exactly for this: the aircraft follows anything publishing MAVLink position data (another vehicle, a ground-based transmitter, or a small telemetry device carried by a person), maintaining a configurable 3D offset from it. All the target needs is a telemetry link and something broadcasting its GPS position — either a standard `GLOBAL_POSITION_INT` MAVLink message or the purpose-built `FOLLOW_TARGET` message. No cameras, no detection model, no geolocation math at all — it's a stock, documented ArduPilot feature with its own tuning parameters. The catch, obviously, is that it only works for a target that's actively cooperating — a crew member's phone running a beacon app, or a small transmitter clipped to a vehicle. For monitoring known, consenting service crews on scheduled visits, this is a dramatically simpler build than anything vision-based, and worth treating as the "easy mode" version of this capability.

**Non-cooperative tracking — the target is only ever seen, never broadcasting anything; you have to detect and locate it yourself.** This is the harder, more interesting case, and the one that actually needs the AI + geolocation pipeline you're asking about — useful for exactly the case a beacon can't cover: an unrecognized vehicle or person you weren't told to expect.

## 2. The math: it's single-camera geolocation with recursive filtering, not two-observer triangulation

Worth a terminology correction that also simplifies the picture: what you actually need isn't triangulation in the classic sense (two separate observation points solving for an intersection). It's **single-ray ground geolocation**, done from one moving camera, refined over time by fusing repeated observations — a well-published technique in UAV surveillance research. The pipeline, as described in a 2025 study that flight-tested exactly this (moving ground target tracking from a gimbal camera on a UAV):

You detect the target in the video frame (the paper uses Tiny-YOLO — a lightweight version of the same detector family already recommended in Research Note 4 for your people/vehicle detection work — paired with a visual tracker to keep the target centered between detections). You know the aircraft's position and attitude (from GPS/INS) and the gimbal's pan/tilt angles pointing the camera at the target. Using a standard pinhole camera model and a chain of coordinate-frame rotations (inertial → aircraft body → gimbal), you cast a ray from the camera through the target's pixel location and find where that ray intersects the ground — approximated as a flat plane at the aircraft's known altitude above terrain, or a digital elevation model for more accuracy on uneven ground. That single-frame estimate is noisy on its own, so it's fed into a Kalman-style filter (the paper compares an Extended Kalman Filter and an Unscented Kalman Filter) along with a simple motion model for how a person or vehicle moves, which smooths the position estimate and — importantly — lets you keep tracking through brief occlusions when the target is temporarily out of view. The paper reports the more sophisticated filter (UKF) cutting position error by about a third compared to the simpler one specifically during occlusion events, and the whole pipeline ran in real time on embedded hardware in actual flight tests, tracking targets from roughly 140 m standoff distance. That's a strong existence proof: this isn't a research question mark, it's an implementation task with a documented reference design.

One practical implication: this approach works meaningfully better with a **gimbal-stabilized camera** whose pan/tilt angle is known precisely, since the whole geolocation calculation depends on knowing exactly where the camera is pointed relative to the aircraft. A fixed, non-gimbaled downward camera can still work for near-nadir shots with a simpler flat-ground assumption, but you lose accuracy and flexibility (can't keep an off-to-the-side target centered while the aircraft continues on its route) compared to a gimbaled setup.

## 3. Getting the computed position into ArduPilot in real time — yes, this is a standard, documented interface

Once your companion computer has a target position, ArduPilot's **GUIDED mode** is built exactly for this kind of continuous external redirection. The relevant MAVLink messages — `SET_POSITION_TARGET_GLOBAL_INT` (lat/lon-based) and `SET_POSITION_TARGET_LOCAL_NED` (local frame-based) — let an external computer command position, velocity, or acceleration targets on the fly, and this is officially documented, widely used "offboard control" functionality, not a hack. The one hard constraint to design around: if you're sending velocity or acceleration commands, ArduPilot expects a fresh command roughly every second and will stop the aircraft if none arrives within about 3 seconds — meaning your detect → geolocate → command loop needs to run reliably faster than that. In practice this is an easy bar to clear; YOLO-class detectors run at several to tens of frames per second on Jetson-class companion computers, well inside that budget.

There's also a closely related, already-packaged ArduPilot feature that's worth studying as a reference implementation even though it solves a narrower problem: **Precision Landing**. It's the exact same architectural pattern — a companion computer (often a Raspberry Pi with a downward camera, using an IR beacon via IR-LOCK hardware, or a simple ArUco marker with plain OpenCV) detects a target visually, computes its position relative to the aircraft, and streams that via the MAVLink `LANDING_TARGET` message so ArduPilot corrects the approach in real time. It's a much simpler case (final approach to a known marker, not tracking an arbitrary moving person across a wide area), but it's a fully documented, community-proven demonstration that "companion computer sees something, tells ArduPilot where it is, ArduPilot flies there" works reliably on this exact platform — a good starting point to learn the MAVLink integration pattern before tackling the harder moving-target case.

## 4. What this means for scoping the thesis

Given everything above, there's a natural difficulty ladder worth laying out explicitly rather than jumping straight to the hardest version: precision-landing-style fixed-marker tracking is the easiest way to learn and validate the companion-computer-to-ArduPilot MAVLink pipeline; cooperative Follow Mode with a GPS beacon gets you a working "track a known crew" capability with essentially none of the computer-vision or geolocation complexity; and full non-cooperative visual detection-and-follow (YOLO + gimbal geolocation + Kalman filtering + GUIDED-mode setpoints) is the hardest and most impressive version, and the one that actually delivers the "notice and respond to something unexpected" value that makes this more than a scheduled-visit tracker. None of these are mutually exclusive — a reasonable thesis arc is to build the pipeline in roughly that order, demonstrating increasing capability rather than betting the whole year on the hardest version working first try.
