# UAV FP — Research Note 8: Pivoting to AI-Driven Flying Wing Design Optimization

*Compiled 13 August 2026. Reflects a scope shift from navigation/tracking/geolocation toward an AI model that optimizes flying-wing design, then builds the result.*

## Short answer

This is not only feasible, it's arguably a better-fitting thesis for a mechanical engineering team than the autonomy-heavy version — there's a direct, well-published academic precedent for exactly this idea (ANN + genetic algorithm optimizing a flying wing glider's aerodynamic design), free tools already used by students for this specific task, and a mature, low-cost manufacturing path (CNC hot-wire foam cutting) that turns an optimized wing geometry directly into a flyable physical part. It also doesn't have to be a clean break from everything in Research Notes 1–7 — worth deciding deliberately whether the flying wing you're optimizing is still sized around the oil & gas monitoring mission (range, payload, endurance) or a standalone aerodynamics exercise; both are legitimate, but they lead to different framing for your professor.

## 1. This exact idea is already published, more than once

A 2025 paper — "Aerodynamic Design Optimization for Flying Wing Gliders Based on the Combination of Artificial Neural Networks and Genetic Algorithms" — is about as close a match to your new direction as you'll find in the literature. The broader pattern (an ANN trained as a fast stand-in for aerodynamic simulation, with a genetic algorithm searching the design space using that stand-in instead of running full simulations for every candidate) shows up repeatedly and isn't unique to flying wings: "UAV aerodynamic design involving genetic algorithm and artificial neural network for wing preliminary computation" and "Optimization of Aerodynamic Design of a Winged UAV Through Genetic Algorithms and Large Eddy Simulation" cover the same territory for UAV wings generally. This combination — often called surrogate-based optimization — is the standard, well-understood way this class of problem gets solved in both academic and industry aerospace design work, not a novel research risk.

**Why the surrogate model matters, in practical terms:** running a full CFD simulation (or even a lower-fidelity panel-method analysis) for every candidate wing design in a search over hundreds or thousands of variations would be slow. Training a neural network to approximate "given these design parameters, predict lift, drag, stability derivatives" lets the genetic algorithm evaluate candidates in milliseconds instead, making a broad automated search of the design space actually practical on a laptop rather than a cluster.

## 2. The tools to do this are free, and there's direct student-level precedent

**XFLR5** is the standard free tool for exactly this kind of work, and is specifically popular in the flying-wing community (it's commonly used to evaluate the reflexed airfoils — Eppler, MH-series — that flying wings need for pitch stability without a tail). It implements the Vortex Lattice Method (VLM), a panel-method-class aerodynamic solver that's dramatically faster than full CFD while still being accurate enough for conceptual/preliminary design. A documented student project (AeroEd) doing wing planform optimization — varying taper ratio, aspect ratio, and washout twist across 50+ configurations, batch-run through XFLR5's VLM via Python scripts, optimizing L/D subject to a stall-margin constraint — explicitly notes that this approach delivers "hundreds of design evaluations per hour on a laptop," and frames it as solid, standard undergraduate-level aerodynamics work. That's a strong signal this is genuinely achievable within your remaining timeline, not a stretch goal.

For a more complete conceptual-design framework rather than building all the optimization glue code yourselves, **SUAVE** (Stanford, open source, LGPL-licensed, built in Python on NumPy/SciPy/scikit-learn) is a full conceptual aircraft design and optimization environment explicitly built to handle "unconventional" designs, including UAVs — it's free, actively used in academia, and already structured for exactly this kind of design-optimization workflow rather than something you'd build from scratch. **OpenVSP** (NASA, open source) is the standard companion tool for parametric 3D geometry generation and is commonly paired with both XFLR5-class solvers and SUAVE.

## 3. "Then builds it" — this is a realistic, well-precedented manufacturing path, not a stretch

Flying wings are one of the easiest UAV airframe types to manufacture from a computed geometry, because the dominant hobbyist/professional method — **4-axis CNC hot-wire foam cutting** — takes airfoil coordinates plus planform geometry (root/tip chord, sweep, twist) as direct input and cuts a foam wing core to that exact shape. This isn't a niche or expensive process: there are multiple fully open-source 4-axis hot-wire cutter designs (Arduino + RAMPS-based builds documented on GitHub, in the range of a few hundred dollars in parts), well within reach of a student team to build themselves or access through a university makerspace or local RC club. In other words, the output of your optimization pipeline (a specific airfoil + planform geometry) maps almost directly onto the input format this manufacturing method needs — a genuinely short path from "the AI says this is the optimal shape" to "here's the physical wing."

If you want the structural design (spars, ribs, internal layout) to also be part of the optimized/AI-driven output rather than just the external aerodynamic shape, there's a separate but related body of published work on topology-optimized, 3D-printed UAV structural components — including topology-optimized wing spars 3D printed with continuous carbon-fiber reinforcement, and fully topology-optimized/3D-printed small UAV airframes validated with real flight testing. That's a heavier lift than the aerodynamic-shape optimization alone, but it's a legitimate "phase 2" if the aerodynamic optimization pipeline comes together faster than expected and you want to extend scope rather than sit idle.

## 4. Worth deciding explicitly: does this still serve the oil & gas mission, or is it a standalone exercise?

This is the one open question this pivot raises that's worth resolving before your meeting, because it changes how you frame the thesis. Research Notes 1–7 built a specific mission context — range needs for covering dispersed Vaca Muerta wellsites, payload budget for an RGB/thermal camera, the case for a fixed-wing or flying-wing airframe specifically because of endurance (Note 7's 18650 battery discussion already pointed this direction). If the flying wing you're now optimizing is still sized and constrained by that mission (optimize for maximum range at a defined payload weight, cruise speed suited to the monitoring task, etc.), this pivot is really a *deepening* of the existing project — the applied, sponsor-facing story from Notes 1–4 stays fully intact, and the AI/optimization work becomes the specific technical contribution that gets you there. If instead the goal is a more general-purpose flying-wing optimization exercise decoupled from a specific mission, that's also a perfectly legitimate mechanical engineering thesis, but it changes the pitch — less "here's a product a sponsor would pay for," more "here's a generalizable design methodology." Both are defensible; picking one deliberately (rather than leaving it ambiguous) will make the meeting go more smoothly.

## Net assessment

Compared to the navigation/tracking/geolocation direction from Research Notes 5–6, this pivot trades a software/robotics-heavy build (companion computers, MAVLink, computer vision, Kalman filters — genuinely outside a typical mechanical engineering skill set) for a problem that sits much closer to your team's actual training: aerodynamics, optimization, structures, and manufacturing. It's also lower-risk in the sense that the tools (XFLR5, SUAVE, OpenVSP), the technique (ANN/surrogate + genetic algorithm), and the manufacturing method (hot-wire foam CNC) are all mature, free or cheap, and already demonstrated at student-project scale — you're assembling proven pieces rather than inventing new ones.
